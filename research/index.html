Creatine monohydrate

Performance Strong
What it does

Creatine is stored in muscle as phosphocreatine, a fast fuel reserve your body leans on during short, hard efforts like sprints, jumps and heavy sets.

What the research shows
  • The International Society of Sports Nutrition (ISSN) reviewed the evidence and concluded that creatine raises the creatine stored in muscle, which may explain gains in high-intensity exercise performance and in training adaptations.[1]
  • The same position stand found supplementation safe and well tolerated in healthy people. Daily doses much higher than 5 g were studied for years without safety concerns.[1]
  • A 2022 meta-analysis of 23 studies on creatine and muscle damage found a mixed picture. Markers of muscle damage were lower 48 to 90 hours after a single hard session, but higher 24 hours after sessions at the end of several weeks of supplementing. Differences in soreness and inflammation were not statistically significant.[6]
What to know. The position stand lists recovery and injury prevention as possible benefits, not settled ones, and the muscle damage meta-analysis found mixed results. The performance evidence is the strongest part of the case for creatine. Many of the recovery studies used loading doses (around 20 g a day), not a daily 5 g.[6]
2g

Beta-alanine

Performance Moderate at higher doses
What it does

Beta-alanine is the building block your body uses to make carnosine, a compound in muscle that helps buffer the acid that builds up during hard efforts.

What the research shows
  • The ISSN position stand reports that about 4 to 6 g a day for at least two to four weeks raises muscle carnosine and improves exercise performance, with the clearest gains in efforts lasting roughly one to four minutes.[2]
  • Tingling is the only side effect reported. Smaller divided doses (about 1.6 g) or sustained-release forms reduce it.[2]
  • A 2017 meta-analysis of 40 studies and 1,461 participants found a small overall benefit. The effect was larger for exercise capacity (how long you can keep going) than for performance tests, and the total amount of beta-alanine taken did not significantly change the size of the effect.[7]
  • In a placebo-controlled study of 15 trained 400-meter sprinters, 4.8 g a day for four weeks raised muscle carnosine by about 47% in the soleus and 37% in the gastrocnemius, and torque held up better in the later bouts of repeated hard efforts.[8]
What to know. The studied range is a daily total, built up over weeks. Each QUBE has 2 g, so two a day reaches the low end of that range. The position stand also says more research is needed on strength and on efforts longer than about 25 minutes.[2]
1,000mg

Tart cherry extract

Recovery · Inflammation Mixed
What it does

Tart cherries are rich in anthocyanins, the pigments that give them their deep red color. Their proposed antioxidant and anti-inflammatory effects are why they are studied for exercise recovery. QUBE uses an extract standardized for anthocyanins.[3]

What the research shows
  • A 2026 review of the literature found benefits show up more often for muscle soreness and how recovered you feel than for strength, power or blood markers. Several controlled trials found no effect.[3]
  • The authors suggest tart cherry as a tool for periods of high recovery demand, such as congested schedules, short turnarounds and travel. They also note that long-term data are limited.[3]
  • A 2021 meta-analysis of 14 studies found a small benefit for muscle soreness, moderate benefits for recovery of strength and power, and small reductions in CRP and IL-6 (blood markers of inflammation). It found no significant effect on creatine kinase or TNF-alpha.[4]
  • A 2021 systematic review of 25 studies on tart cherry and pomegranate concluded that benefits are more likely when supplementation starts some days before hard exercise and continues for some days after, for at least 8 to 10 days in total, and when daily intake provides at least 1,000 mg of total phenolic compounds.[9]
What to know. Reviews agree that tart cherry can help with soreness and recovery of function, but results vary with the product, dose and timing. The 1,000 mg threshold above refers to total phenolic compounds, not the weight of an extract, so the amount of extract in a product is not directly comparable to the studies.[9]

Curcumin

Inflammation · Recovery Mixed
What it does

Curcumin is the main active compound in turmeric. It is studied for its role in the inflammation and muscle soreness that follow hard training.

What the research shows
  • A 2022 meta-analysis of 10 randomized trials found that curcumin lowered creatine kinase (a marker of muscle damage), muscle soreness and TNF-alpha, and improved strength and range of motion after hard exercise. It found no significant change in IL-6 or IL-8.[10]
  • A 2024 systematic review of 11 studies in people who exercise regularly concluded that curcumin may reduce muscle symptoms after eccentric exercise, especially soreness, and that taking it after exercise appeared more beneficial.[11]
  • A 2020 review noted that results are conflicting, with some studies finding less soreness and others finding no effect. It describes typical daily doses of 150 to 1,500 mg.[12]
What to know. Results across studies are conflicting, with some finding less soreness and others finding no effect. Studies used a range of doses and timing, and many were small, so treat the findings as promising rather than settled.[12]
Na · K · Mg

Electrolytes

Hydration Varies by mineral
What they do

Sodium, potassium and magnesium are minerals your body uses for fluid balance and for the electrical signals that run your muscles and nerves. You lose some in sweat.

What the research shows
  • A meta-analysis of 14 randomized trials found that magnesium supplementation did not significantly improve strength, power or peak torque overall. Benefits appeared in older adults and heavy drinkers, groups more likely to be low in magnesium, and not in athletes with adequate magnesium status.[5]
  • A 2017 review of animal and human studies describes magnesium's role in energy metabolism and normal muscle function. It reports that the human evidence on performance is limited and mixed, with several supplementation trials at 122 to 350 mg a day finding no improvement.[13]
What to know. Read this as: magnesium is part of a balanced electrolyte base, not a performance booster for well-nourished athletes.[5]
