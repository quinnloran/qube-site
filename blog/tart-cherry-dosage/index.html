If you have looked up tart cherry dosage, you have probably seen a dozen different numbers. Juice ounces, capsules, milligrams of extract, milligrams of anthocyanins. Here is what the research actually used, and what it admits it cannot tell you.

What is tart cherry in a supplement?

Tart cherry supplements come from sour cherries, usually Montmorency, as juice, concentrate or powdered extract. They are rich in polyphenols, including anthocyanins, the pigments that give the fruit its deep red color. Those compounds are why researchers study tart cherry for exercise recovery.[2]

For the recovery evidence itself, see tart cherry for muscle soreness: what studies show. This post is about the numbers: how much, how long, and what the label should tell you.

What tart cherry dosage did studies use?

Studies varied a lot. In a review of 43 human studies, total polyphenol doses ranged from 143 to 2,140 mg a day and anthocyanin doses from 15 to 547 mg a day. The forms ranged from juice and concentrate to capsules.[2]

In the exercise studies that review tabulated, a common pattern was a small serving twice a day (for example 30 mL of concentrate, or 236 mL of juice) for about a week to 12 days. A few used a single daily capsule. One marathon study used 236 mL twice a day for 8 days, estimated at about 80 mg of anthocyanins a day, and the review's table lists it as improved recovery.[2]

Those doses are what researchers studied. They are not a prescription, and they are not a promise about what any product will do for you.

Is there a best tart cherry dosage?

Not yet. The 2021 review concluded that the relative efficacy of different doses cannot be reliably determined. Results did not follow a clear dose-response pattern: one study at 547 mg of anthocyanins a day found improved recovery, while another at the same figure found no performance benefit.[2]

The bigger picture is still encouraging. A separate meta-analysis of 14 studies found tart cherry supplementation was associated with a small benefit for muscle soreness and a moderate benefit for strength recovery, with small effects on the inflammation markers C-reactive protein and interleukin-6. It found no significant effect on creatine kinase. The authors concluded tart cherry can aid some aspects of recovery from strenuous exercise.[1]

So the honest summary: tart cherry has research behind it for recovery, but nobody has pinned down the ideal amount.

How long should you take tart cherry?

Most exercise studies tabulated in the 2021 review started tart cherry 4 to 7 days before the hard session and continued afterward, for a total of roughly 6 to 12 days. They tested a routine, not a one-time dose right before training.[2]

That fits how QUBE is built. It is a daily powder you take as part of your morning routine, not a pre-workout. If you want to see how the same ingredient looks for another goal, read tart cherry for sleep: what the research shows.

How do you read a tart cherry label?

Look for what the extract is standardized to, not just a weight. Cherries vary by batch, and the 2021 review found that only 20 of 43 studies analyzed their own supplement. A milligram count of "cherry" alone does not tell you the anthocyanin content.[2]

Questions worth asking about any tart cherry product:

  • Is it juice, concentrate or extract?
  • Does the label say what the extract is standardized to, such as anthocyanins?
  • Is the serving something you will actually take every day?

FAQ

What is the best tart cherry dosage for recovery?

No one can name a single best amount yet. A 2021 review of 43 studies found anthocyanin doses from 15 to 547 mg a day and could not reliably say which dose works best. Check with a doctor or athletic trainer for your situation.

How long before exercise should you take tart cherry?

Most exercise studies in the review started tart cherry several days ahead of the hard session (usually 4 to 7 days) and kept going afterward. The research looked at multi-day routines, not a single dose right before training.

Is tart cherry juice the same as tart cherry extract?

Not necessarily. Juice, concentrate and powdered extract differ in polyphenol content, and the review found that only 20 of 43 studies measured what was actually in their supplement. Look for a label that says what the extract is standardized to.

Why does standardization matter on a tart cherry label?

Natural cherries vary by batch, so two products with the same weight on the label can carry different amounts of active compounds. A standard such as anthocyanin content tells you what the extract is measured against.
