Electrolytes for athletes: what the research says

By Quinn, Founder of QUBE

Published Oct 19, 2026Updated Oct 19, 2026Hydration6 minute read

Key takeaways

  • Sweat sodium loss varies a lot between athletes and between days, so one-size-fits-all numbers are shaky.[1]
  • After hard sessions, adding sodium helps the body keep the fluid you drink, which matters most when you train again soon.[2]
  • A 2013 meta-analysis found dehydration up to 4% of body weight was very unlikely to hurt time-trial performance in endurance studies.[3]
  • QUBE includes a balanced blend of sodium, potassium and magnesium for the hydration pillar.

Every locker room has an opinion on electrolytes. I wanted to know what the research actually says, so I read it. The short version: electrolytes for athletes matter most when you sweat a lot or have to turn around and train again, and the studies are more careful than the marketing.

Do electrolytes for athletes actually matter?

Sometimes. For a short, easy session, water and normal meals usually cover it. When you sweat heavily or have another session coming, the research suggests the minerals in what you drink affect how much fluid your body keeps.[2]

That "it depends" is the honest answer, and the rest of this post explains what it depends on.

What are electrolytes, and which ones do athletes lose in sweat?

Electrolytes are minerals that carry an electrical charge in body fluids. The main ones for athletes are sodium, potassium and magnesium, which your body uses for fluid balance and for the signals that run muscles and nerves.

Sweat is mostly water, but it carries minerals too, and sodium is the one researchers track most closely.[1]

How much sodium do athletes lose in sweat?

It varies widely. A 2017 review found that sweat rate and sweat sodium concentration differ between individuals and also change within the same person based on exercise intensity, environment, heat acclimation, fitness, diet and hydration.[1]

The review's takeaway is practical: sweat testing can help guide fluid and electrolyte replacement, but only if it is collected and interpreted properly. A teammate who barely sweats and a teammate who ends practice with salt on their shirt do not need the same plan.

Should you drink to thirst or follow a plan?

A 2013 meta-analysis of 15 articles (122 subjects) found that in time-trial protocols, dehydration did not significantly change performance. Only fixed-intensity lab protocols showed a drop, about 1.9%. The author argued athletes can drink according to thirst.[3]

Those were endurance studies, not a basketball game in a hot gym. So treat it as a reason not to panic about every ounce, not as a rule for your sport. Your athletic trainer knows your conditions better than a meta-analysis does.

Do electrolytes help you rehydrate after practice?

Sodium appears to help. A 2017 review of post-exercise rehydration says replacement volume should exceed what you lost, that large amounts of plain water cause a prompt diuresis (more urine), and that adding sodium helps maintain fluid balance.[2]

This matters most for athletes who finish a session dehydrated and play or train again the same day: tournaments, two-a-days, travel weekends. The review also notes that carbohydrate and protein may play a role, and that more research is needed on ideal amounts.

What about magnesium for athletes?

Magnesium is a cofactor in energy metabolism, and a 2017 review reports that the body's need for it increases with physical activity. It also describes links between magnesium status and measures like grip strength, leg power and jumping.[4]

That does not mean more is better. If you already get enough, this research does not promise extra performance. Ask a doctor or athletic trainer before changing how much magnesium you take.

FAQ

Do athletes need electrolytes or is water enough?

It depends on how much you sweat and how soon you train again. For a short, easy session, water and a normal meal usually cover it. When you sweat heavily or have another session soon, sodium helps the body hold on to the fluid you drink.

How much sodium do athletes lose in sweat?

It varies a lot from person to person and from day to day. A 2017 review found sweat rate and sweat sodium concentration change with intensity, climate, heat acclimation, fitness and diet, so there is no single number that fits every athlete.

Should I drink to thirst during a game?

A 2013 meta-analysis found dehydration up to 4% of body weight was very unlikely to hurt time-trial performance, and the author argued athletes can drink according to thirst. Those were endurance studies, so ask your athletic trainer how it applies to your sport and conditions.

Does magnesium improve athletic performance?

A 2017 review describes magnesium as a cofactor in energy metabolism and reports that the need for it rises with physical activity. It also notes links between magnesium status and strength and power measures. It does not show that extra magnesium improves performance in athletes who already get enough.

Is QUBE a sports drink?

No. QUBE is a daily powder you mix into 12 to 16 oz of water once a day as part of your morning routine. Its sodium, potassium and magnesium blend supports the hydration pillar, but it does not replace drinking fluids during long or hot sessions.
