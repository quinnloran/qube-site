Creatine for endurance athletes: does it help?

By Quinn, Founder of QUBE

Published Nov 14, 2026Updated Nov 14, 2026Ingredients8 minute read

Key takeaways

  • Creatine is best studied for short, hard efforts, so the case for creatine for endurance athletes is narrower and more mixed.[1][2]
  • A 2023 review says creatine appears to increase time to exhaustion in high-intensity endurance work, while time-trial results are mixed.[1]
  • The best fit looks like events decided by surges and end spurts, such as cycling and triathlon.[1][2]
  • The trade-off is body mass: about 0.9 kg in the 2026 review, which may hurt weight-bearing efforts like running.[2]

I play basketball, which is a sport of repeated surges, not one steady pace. Still, plenty of runners, cyclists and swimmers on campus ask me about creatine for endurance athletes, because creatine has a reputation as a power supplement. I read the two newest reviews on the topic. The short answer: it is context-dependent, and the research is more cautious than the gym version of the story.

Does creatine help endurance athletes?

Sometimes, but not reliably. A 2023 review says creatine appears to increase time to exhaustion in high-intensity endurance work, yet time-trial results are mixed. A 2026 review says creatine did not reliably improve endurance performance in pooled analyses, and called neither creatine nor beta-alanine a universal ergogenic aid.[1][2]

The 2023 review defines endurance performance as cyclical, large-muscle activities lasting more than about 3 minutes.[1] Both reviews are narrative reviews, meaning the authors summarize the literature instead of pooling every trial into one number. The 2026 authors also note that most evidence comes from untrained or recreationally active people, so it says less about highly trained athletes.[2]

Why might creatine help in surges and end spurts?

Creatine raises muscle phosphocreatine, which supports faster ATP resynthesis and acid buffering. The 2023 review says creatine appears to extend time to exhaustion likely by increasing anaerobic work capacity, so it seems most useful when a race depends on repeated surges or a finishing sprint.[1]

That matches what the 2026 review reports from trials: improved late-race sprint power in a 120 km cycling time trial in elite cyclists, and better interval power in triathletes. It also lists a possible glycogen resynthesis benefit when creatine is taken with carbohydrates.[2] The 2023 review describes this as a possibility, not a settled result.[1]

Does creatine body mass gain hurt runners?

It can, in weight-bearing sports. Both reviews flag body mass as the main downside. The 2026 review says creatine can add about 0.9 kg, which may impair efforts such as running, and the 2023 review says the extra mass may offset benefits in weight-bearing activities.[1][2]

If your event has you carrying your own weight uphill for an hour, that is a real trade-off to weigh. If your bike or boat supports your weight, the trade-off looks smaller, though the reviews do not quantify it for every sport.

Which endurance sports might benefit most?

The 2023 review suggests creatine may be beneficial for cross-country skiing, mountain biking, cycling and triathlon, plus short events where end spurts are critical, such as rowing, kayaking and track cycling.[1] The evidence for distance running is thinner.

How much creatine do endurance athletes take?

The 2026 review describes 3 to 5 g a day for about 4 weeks, or a loading phase of about 20 g a day for 5 to 7 days followed by 3 to 5 g a day.[2] A 2021 expert review says doses of about 3 to 5 g a day are generally well tolerated, and the ISSN calls creatine safe and well tolerated in healthy individuals.[3][4]

Daily consistency is the point. For the timing question, read when to take creatine, and for whether you need the heavy first week, creatine loading. Worried about fluids on long, hot days? See does creatine dehydrate you.

What about creatine and beta-alanine for endurance?

No clear synergy. The 2026 review found no clear added benefit from combining creatine and beta-alanine for aerobic capacity or thresholds. It describes beta-alanine benefits as small and mostly near the aerobic-anaerobic transition, at studied amounts of about 3.2 to 6.4 g a day for at least 4 weeks.[2]

That studied beta-alanine amount is higher than the 2 g in one QUBE, so I do not apply those results to QUBE's dose. For the stacking question in general, see creatine and beta-alanine together.

FAQ

Does creatine help endurance athletes?

Sometimes, in specific situations. A 2023 review says creatine appears to extend time to exhaustion in high-intensity endurance work, but time-trial results are mixed. A 2026 review says pooled analyses did not reliably show better endurance performance, with some trials showing gains in late-race sprint power.

Is creatine good for runners?

The evidence is thinner for runners. Creatine can add about 0.9 kg of body mass, which the 2026 review says may impair weight-bearing efforts such as running. Runners whose races hinge on a finishing kick or repeated surges may be the best fit, and individual response varies.

Does creatine help cyclists?

Cycling shows more positive signals. The 2026 review points to better late-race sprint power in a 120 km time trial in elite cyclists and better interval power in triathletes in some trials. The 2023 review lists cycling among sports where creatine may be beneficial.

How much creatine should endurance athletes take?

The 2026 review describes about 3 to 5 g a day for roughly 4 weeks, or an optional loading phase of about 20 g a day for 5 to 7 days followed by 3 to 5 g a day. Healthy people tolerate 3 to 5 g a day well, per a 2021 expert review.

Should I take creatine and beta-alanine together for endurance?

The 2026 review found no clear added benefit from combining them for aerobic capacity or thresholds. Studied beta-alanine amounts were about 3.2 to 6.4 g a day for at least 4 weeks, more than the 2 g in one QUBE, and effects were modest.
