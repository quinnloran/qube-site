Creatine and Beta-Alanine Together: Does It Work?

Two of the most-asked questions I hear from teammates are about creatine and beta-alanine together: do they stack, and is the pair worth it? Here is what the research actually says, in plain English, including the parts that are not proven.

Can you take creatine and beta-alanine together?

Yes. Creatine and beta-alanine are commonly combined, and a 2025 systematic review of randomized trials found the pair may support high-intensity exercise performance. The two work in different ways, so they are not doing the same job twice.[1]

That review looked at 7 randomized controlled trials with 263 participants, all comparing the combination against either supplement alone for at least four weeks. It is a useful starting point, and it is also a small body of evidence, which I will come back to.

What does each one do?

Creatine raises the creatine stored in muscle, which may explain better high-intensity performance and training adaptations. Beta-alanine raises muscle carnosine, which acts as an intracellular pH buffer, a way of handling the acid that builds up in hard efforts.[2][3]

Put simply, creatine is about the power for short, hard efforts, and beta-alanine is about keeping hard efforts going. That is why both sit in QUBE's performance pillar. You can see the full breakdown on the Research page, and the creatine side in more depth in our post on creatine for athletes.

What does the research say about the combination?

The 2025 systematic review concluded that creatine plus beta-alanine may be effective for high-intensity exercise performance, particularly anaerobic power and repeated-bout performance.[1]

Repeated-bout work is the kind of effort basketball players know well: sprint, recover a little, sprint again. For the single-supplement evidence, a meta-analysis of 40 beta-alanine studies with 1,461 participants found a significant overall benefit.[4] The ISSN adds that beta-alanine effects are more pronounced in efforts lasting about 1 to 4 minutes.[2]

What has the combination not been shown to do?

In the 2025 review, combining the two did not beat either one alone for maximal strength, body composition or aerobic capacity (VO2max, lactate threshold or time to exhaustion). Body composition results were mixed across studies.[1]

I would rather say that plainly than oversell it. Beta-alanine is a performance ingredient, and the pair is not a shortcut to strength. The review also covered only 7 trials, mostly in men (231 of 263 participants), so the evidence is still building.

How much beta-alanine do the studies use?

The ISSN position stand describes 4 to 6 g a day for at least 2 to 4 weeks to raise muscle carnosine and improve exercise performance. One QUBE has 2 g of beta-alanine, which is lower than that studied range, so the research does not tell us QUBE's 2 g does the same thing.[2]

The ISSN also notes that beta-alanine appears safe in healthy people at recommended doses, and that tingling (paresthesia) is the only side effect reported. Smaller divided doses or a sustained-release form can reduce it.[2] Because carnosine builds over weeks, beta-alanine is a daily-habit ingredient, not a one-time boost.

FAQ

Can you take creatine and beta-alanine together?

Yes, they are commonly combined, and the 2025 systematic review found combined use may support high-intensity exercise performance. The two work through different mechanisms. As with any supplement, check with a doctor or athletic trainer first if you have a health condition or take medication.

Does taking creatine and beta-alanine together build more strength than creatine alone?

Not in the reviewed trials. The 2025 systematic review found no greater effect on maximal strength, body composition or aerobic capacity than either supplement alone. The signal was in high-intensity work, especially anaerobic power and repeated efforts.

How much beta-alanine do the studies use?

The ISSN position stand describes 4 to 6 g a day for at least 2 to 4 weeks. One QUBE has 2 g of beta-alanine, so QUBE's amount is lower than that studied range.

Does beta-alanine cause tingling?

Tingling (paresthesia) is the only side effect the ISSN reports for beta-alanine. The position stand notes it can be reduced with smaller divided doses or a sustained-release form.

Is QUBE a pre-workout?
