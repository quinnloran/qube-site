Tart cherry for muscle soreness: what studies show

By Quinn, Founder of QUBE

Published Oct 16, 2026Updated Oct 16, 2026Recovery5 minute read

Key takeaways

  • A 2021 meta-analysis of 14 studies found tart cherry linked to a small reduction in soreness and a moderate benefit for strength recovery.
  • A 2025 meta-analysis of 10 trials found no significant change in soreness scores, but better recovery of muscle strength.
  • The studies used tart cherry juice. Results do not transfer automatically to any powder or extract.
  • Each QUBE has 1,000 mg of tart cherry extract standardized to anthocyanins.

Everyone on my team has heard that tart cherry helps with soreness. I wanted to know if the research backs that up, so I read the pooled studies. The short version: tart cherry for muscle soreness has a mixed record, while its record for getting strength back after hard sessions looks better.

Does tart cherry help muscle soreness?

Maybe a little, and the evidence is not consistent. A 2021 meta-analysis found a small reduction in soreness, but a 2025 meta-analysis found no significant change in soreness scores. Both found more promising results for recovery of strength.[1][2]

Soreness is also a subjective rating, which makes it noisy. The 2025 authors point out that this kind of scale may lack precision.

What is tart cherry, and why do athletes use it?

Tart cherry (often Montmorency) is a cherry rich in anthocyanins, the pigments that give it its deep red color. Athletes use it after hard training because researchers have studied it for markers of exercise-induced muscle damage.

That phrase just means the temporary strength loss, soreness and inflammation that follow unfamiliar or very hard exercise.

What did the two meta-analyses find?

Both pooled analyses point the same direction on strength recovery and split on soreness. Pooling studies matters because single trials are small and often disagree.

The 2021 analysis also reported a large effect on jump height and a small effect on sprint time.[1] The 2025 authors concluded that tart cherry juice "may improve muscle function and some inflammatory biomarkers" after exercise-induced muscle damage, and called for larger, higher-quality studies.[2]

How much tart cherry did the studies use?

The 2025 meta-analysis covered 60 to 1,200 ml of tart cherry juice a day for 7 to 12 days. Its dose analysis suggested about 260 ml a day was best for strength recovery, and it could not identify an ideal amount for soreness.[2]

Participants were physically active adults aged 18 and older, mostly men. That is worth remembering if you are a different age or training load. Check with a doctor or athletic trainer if you have a health condition or take medication.
