Creatine on rest days: should you take it?

By Quinn, Founder of QUBE

Published Nov 17, 2026Updated Nov 17, 2026Ingredients4 minute read

Key takeaways

  • In a 1996 muscle biopsy study of 31 men, muscle creatine fell gradually after supplementing stopped and matched baseline by 30 days later, so one day off is not a reset.[1]
  • I did not find a study that directly compares training days only with every day, so nobody can tell you the exact cost of skipping rest days.
  • A 2021 review says matching creatine timing to your workouts is not supported by solid evidence, which points toward consistency.[2]
  • The body replaces roughly 1 to 3 g of creatine a day, which is why a steady habit keeps stores topped up.[3]

On an off day there is no practice, no lift, and no game. So should you still scoop in creatine on rest days? My teammates ask this constantly. Here is what the research says, and where it runs out.

Should you take creatine on rest days?

Probably yes, because it is the simplest habit, but the research does not prove you must. Muscle creatine builds and fades over weeks, not hours, and I found no study that tested training days only against every day.[1][2]

The studies in this area were built around loading, maintenance and timing, not around rest days. So I will show you what they did test and let you draw the line.

How fast does muscle creatine drop when you stop?

Slowly. In 31 healthy, regularly exercising men, muscle total creatine rose about 20% after 6 days at 20 g a day, then gradually declined once supplementing stopped. By 30 days after stopping, it was no different from the starting level.[1]

That is the heart of the rest-day question. A decline that takes about a month to finish is not erased by a single day off. One caveat: this tested stopping completely, not skipping one day in seven, so it is a clue, not a rest-day trial.

Does creatine timing matter on training days versus rest days?

Not in any way researchers can prove yet. A 2021 review concluded that adapting creatine timing to when you train is not currently supported by solid evidence, and that timing mainly matters, if at all, during the early loading phase.[2]

The studies it reviewed did not even agree on rest days. One had volunteers take creatine on non-training days "according to the volunteer's convenience," while two others dosed on training days only.[2] The same reasoning applies in my post on when to take creatine: the clock matters a lot less than the habit.

How much creatine does your body use each day?

The ISSN position stand's background says the body replaces roughly 1 to 3 g of creatine a day, depending on muscle mass. It adds that larger, intensely training athletes may need 5 to 10 g a day to maintain optimal stores.[3]

Creatine is also one of the most studied supplements, with the ISSN reporting up to 30 g a day for 5 years was safe and well tolerated in the groups studied.[3] If you have a health condition or take medication, check with a doctor or athletic trainer first. FAQ

Should I take creatine on rest days?

The research does not show a penalty for skipping, but no study I found tested training days only against every day. Because muscle creatine fell gradually over about a month after stopping in a 1996 study, one day off is not a reset. Taking it daily is the simplest habit.

Will I lose creatine stores if I skip a day?

Not overnight. In Hultman and colleagues' 1996 study, muscle total creatine gradually declined after men stopped supplementing, and by 30 days later it matched their starting level. That tested stopping fully, not skipping a single day.

Does creatine timing around workouts matter on rest days?

A 2021 review concluded that adapting creatine timing to when you train is not currently supported by solid evidence. Rest days have no workout to time it around, so the review's takeaway points to consistency.

How much creatine does the body replace each day?

The ISSN position stand's background text says the body replaces roughly 1 to 3 g of creatine a day, depending on muscle mass, and that larger, intensely training athletes may need 5 to 10 g a day to maintain stores.
