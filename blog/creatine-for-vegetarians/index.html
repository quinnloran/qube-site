Creatine for vegetarians: what research shows

By Quinn, Founder of QUBE

Published Nov 13, 2026Updated Nov 13, 2026Ingredients4 minute read

Key takeaways

  • In an 8-week trial, vegetarians started with lower muscle creatine than non-vegetarians (117 vs 130 mmol/kg dry mass).[2]
  • On creatine, those vegetarians gained 2.4 kg of lean tissue versus 1.9 kg for non-vegetarians on creatine.[2]
  • A 2020 systematic review of 9 studies found larger muscle creatine gains in vegetarians, but the studies were small and often high risk of bias.[1]
  • The ISSN describes 3 to 5 g a day for maintenance, and no vegetarian-specific dose is settled.[3]

A few guys on my team skip meat, and the question comes up: is creatine for vegetarians any different? I read the research. Short version: vegetarians seem to start lower and respond a bit more, but the studies are small, so I will keep the fine print in view.

Do vegetarians have lower muscle creatine?

In the studies reviewed, yes, on average. In a 2003 trial, vegetarians had baseline total muscle creatine of 117 mmol/kg dry mass versus 130 for non-vegetarians. In another study, omnivores lost about 9.5% of muscle creatine after 21 days on a lacto-ovo vegetarian diet.[1][2]

Creatine is a compound your muscles use for short, hard efforts like sprints, jumps and heavy sets. Muscle creatine means the creatine stored inside muscle, and it is the number most of these studies track.

Does creatine work better for vegetarians?

Possibly. In the 2003 trial, 42 people trained for 8 weeks on creatine or placebo. Vegetarians on creatine gained 2.4 kg of lean tissue versus 1.9 kg for non-vegetarians on creatine, and showed greater gains in muscle creatine and total work.[2]

The authors suggested that people with low starting muscle creatine, such as vegetarians, respond more strongly. In that trial, the lower your starting creatine, the bigger the rise: the correlation was r = -0.77.[2]

What did the 2020 systematic review of vegetarian creatine find?

The review pulled together 9 studies of creatine in vegetarians compared with omnivores. Across them, creatine tended to raise muscle creatine more in vegetarians, with some signs of greater lean mass, strength and memory benefits, but results varied by study.[1]

Two examples: one study found muscle creatine rose 76% in vegetarians versus 35% in omnivores on a loading dose. Another found mean power rose about 5% in both groups, while peak power rose only in omnivores.[1]

What is the fine print on vegetarian creatine research?

The evidence base is small and uneven. Five of the nine studies were rated high risk of bias, groups were often under 20 people, doses and diets (vegan versus lacto-ovo) differed, and some outcomes came from only one or two studies.[1]

So the fair read is "likely a bigger response, not a guaranteed one." Nothing here says omnivores should skip creatine, and these studies did not test QUBE.

How much creatine should vegetarian athletes take?

The ISSN position stand describes 3 to 5 g a day for maintenance. Studies in vegetarians used different amounts, from 1 g a day to loading phases of 20 g a day, so there is no settled vegetarian-specific number.[1][3]

For a closer look at the daily-dose question, read creatine loading, and for the basics see creatine for athletes. If you want to know about specific health questions, check with a doctor or athletic trainer.

FAQ

Do vegetarians have lower muscle creatine?

In one 8-week trial, vegetarians started with lower total muscle creatine than non-vegetarians (117 vs 130 mmol/kg dry mass). In a separate study, omnivores who switched to a lacto-ovo vegetarian diet for 21 days lost about 9.5% of their muscle creatine.

Does creatine work better for vegetarians?

Possibly. In the 2003 trial, vegetarians taking creatine gained more lean tissue (2.4 kg vs 1.9 kg) and total work than non-vegetarians taking it. The 2020 review also reports larger muscle creatine increases in vegetarians, but the studies were small.

Can vegans take creatine?

The research reviewed here included vegetarians, and the 2003 trial included people on lacto-ovo or vegan diets. Whether a specific product is vegan depends on its ingredients and manufacturing, so check the label or ask the brand.

How much creatine do vegetarian athletes need?

The ISSN position stand describes 3 to 5 g a day for maintenance. Studies in vegetarians used different doses, from 1 g a day to loading phases, so no vegetarian-specific dose is settled. Talk with a doctor or athletic trainer about your situation.

Is creatine safe for vegetarians?

The studies in the 2020 review did not set out to compare safety, so this post cannot answer that. For individual health questions, check with a doctor or athletic trainer.
