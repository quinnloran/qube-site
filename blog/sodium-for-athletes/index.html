Sodium for athletes: how much do you really lose?

By Quinn, Founder of QUBE

Published Nov 21, 2026Updated Nov 21, 2026Hydration4 minute read

Key takeaways

  • Sweat sodium loss varies a lot between athletes and day to day, so a single number for everyone does not exist.
  • The simplest way to measure your own sweat rate is body mass before and after a session.
  • Whether eating more salt changes how salty your sweat is is still uncertain.
  • Sodium as a cause of cramps and hyponatremia is a theory, not a settled finding.

Sodium for athletes is one of those topics with a lot of confident numbers floating around. Here is what the published reviews actually say, and where they say "we are not sure."

What is sweat sodium loss?

Sweat is mostly water, but it carries electrolytes, and sodium is the main one. Sweat sodium loss is the sodium concentration of your sweat multiplied by how much you sweat. Both pieces change from athlete to athlete.[1]

That is why a teammate can finish the same practice with a salt-streaked jersey while you do not. Sweat sodium concentration and sweat rate are two separate dials.

Why does sweat sodium loss vary so much?

A 2017 review by Baker found that sweating rate and sweat sodium concentration vary considerably within and among individuals. It lists exercise intensity, climate, heat acclimation, fitness, body size, sex, age, diet and hydration status as factors.[1]

The same review notes that sweat tests can be unreliable when methods are not standardized, since collection method, timing and sample handling all change the result. Treat any single number you see online, including a "typical" one, as a rough starting point at best. Note that the author works at a sports drink company research institute, so I use the review only for what it says about variability.

How can an athlete estimate how much they sweat?

According to Baker, the simplest and most accurate method to assess whole-body sweat rate is the change in body mass during exercise. Weigh yourself before and after, and account for fluids you drank and sweat trapped in your clothing.[1]

This tells you about your fluid loss, not your sodium loss. Measuring sweat sodium itself takes a lab or field test, and the review stresses that results depend on method. If you want a number that is yours, ask your athletic trainer or team dietitian.

Does eating more salt change how much sodium is in your sweat?

The evidence is mixed. A 2018 systematic review of six studies found two with a significant diet effect on sweat sodium concentration and two with none, and concluded the effect remains uncertain.[2]

Intakes in those six studies ranged widely, from under 196 mg to 9,177 mg per day, and the studies lasted 3 to 42 days. The authors also flagged limits in how sweat was sampled. So nobody can promise that adding or cutting salt will change your sweat in a predictable way.

Does low sodium cause cramps or hyponatremia?

Not proven. A 2022 systematic review reports that sodium intake, both high and low, has been associated with health and performance issues in endurance athletes. It describes sodium imbalance as a theory for exercise-associated muscle cramps and hyponatremia.[3]

Its main recommendation is that sodium and fluid intake should be considered together. That review focused on endurance and ultra-endurance sport, so do not assume it maps neatly onto a basketball practice. Hyponatremia is a medical issue, so bring any symptoms to a doctor or athletic trainer. FAQ

How much sodium do athletes lose in sweat?

It varies widely between athletes and even for the same athlete from day to day. A 2017 review lists exercise intensity, climate, heat acclimation, fitness, body size, sex, age, diet and hydration status as sources of that variability, so no single number fits everyone.

How can I tell how much I sweat?

The simplest and most accurate method is the change in body mass during exercise, according to a 2017 review. Weigh yourself before and after a session, and ask your athletic trainer how to account for fluids you drank and for sweat trapped in your clothing.

Does eating more salt change how salty your sweat is?

Unclear. A 2018 systematic review of six studies found two with a significant diet effect on sweat sodium concentration and two with none (two were not analyzed statistically). The authors concluded the effect remains uncertain.

Do low sodium levels cause muscle cramps?

That is a theory, not a settled finding. A 2022 systematic review describes sodium imbalance as a proposed contributor to exercise-associated muscle cramps and hyponatremia, and says both sodium and fluid intake deserve attention. See our post on magnesium and cramps for more.
