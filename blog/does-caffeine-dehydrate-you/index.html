Does Caffeine Dehydrate You? What Research Shows

If you have ever skipped your morning coffee before practice because someone said it dries you out, you have run into one of the most common hydration rumors in sports. The question does caffeine dehydrate you has a decent research answer, and it is less dramatic than the locker room version. Here is what one major meta-analysis found, and where it stops.

Does caffeine dehydrate you?

Not in the way the myth suggests. A 2014 meta-analysis of 16 studies found that caffeine raised urine volume by about 109 mL (16%) on average, with a clear effect at rest and no statistically significant effect in active conditions. The authors called the diuretic effect minor.[1]

The review pooled 28 comparisons from 379 healthy adults (246 men and 133 women). The caffeine doses had a median of 300 mg, ranging from 114 to 741 mg, and came from pills, tea, coffee and caffeinated drinks.[1] That is a lot of variety, which is useful for a big-picture answer and less useful for pinning down any one drink.

Does exercise change how caffeine affects urine output?

Yes, in this data the picture changed with activity. Caffeine raised urine volume by about 120 mL (21%) at rest, a significant result, while the active conditions showed about 100 mL (11%) with a confidence interval that included zero, so it was not statistically significant.[1]

The authors concluded the diuretic effect was negated by exercise and that fluid-loss concerns were unwarranted, particularly when caffeine is taken before exercise.[1] I would read that as reassurance, not a green light to stop paying attention to fluids.

What can this research not tell you?

It cannot tell you how caffeine affects your hydration in a hot practice, during travel, or over a long season. The meta-analysis measured urine volume in healthy adults, which is a stand-in for fluid balance, not a direct measure of how hydrated you are on game day.[1]

Two more caveats. The caffeine dose did not explain the differences between studies in the authors' analysis, so I cannot give you a safe number of cups from this paper.[1] And one author is listed as a consultant for a caffeinated chewing gum company, which is worth knowing when you weigh the conclusions.[1] If you have a heart condition or take medication, ask a doctor about caffeine directly.

What does this mean for your daily routine?

The practical read is modest. Based on this one meta-analysis, a normal amount of caffeine does not look like a hydration problem for healthy athletes who exercise, and the people with the clearest urine effect were resting. Your hydration still depends on sweat, sodium and your climate, so I would not treat coffee as a replacement for water. FAQ

Does caffeine dehydrate you?

Not in the way the myth says. A 2014 meta-analysis found caffeine raised urine volume by about 109 mL on average, and the effect was not clearly present during exercise. Ask your athletic trainer about your own situation.

Does coffee count toward my water for practice?

The meta-analysis included coffee, tea, pills and caffeinated drinks, and its authors called the diuretic effect minor. Water is still the simple default, and your trainer can help set a plan for your sport.

Does exercise change how caffeine affects urine output?

In that meta-analysis the effect was significant at rest (about 120 mL more urine) but not significant in active conditions (about 100 mL, with a confidence interval that crossed zero).

Does caffeine affect men and women the same way?

In the pooled data, the urine effect was larger in females than in males, but this is an average across mixed studies, so treat it as a pattern to watch, not a rule.

Should I drink extra water if I have caffeine?

The research I read does not set a catch-up number. Drink to your normal training plan and check with a doctor or athletic trainer about your own needs.
