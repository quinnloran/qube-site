How Much Water Should Athletes Drink?

Every athlete gets told to drink more water, and almost nobody gets told how much. So how much water should athletes drink before, during and after practice? The honest answer is that it depends on your sweat, and the best position statements say exactly that. Here is what the research supports, what it does not, and how I think about it as a D-III basketball player.

How much water should athletes drink?

The National Athletic Trainers' Association (NATA) gives no single daily number. It says to start exercise well hydrated, keep body mass loss under 2 percent during exercise, and avoid gaining weight, since drinking more than you sweat raises the risk of low blood sodium.[1]

The NATA statement is a 2017 position statement written by a panel of researchers and clinicians. It describes starting exercise euhydrated, meaning within about 1 percent of your normal body mass, and suggests that roughly 200 mL every 15 to 20 minutes may be ideal during exercise for many people.[1] That is a starting point for experimenting, not a prescription. Your sweat rate, the weather and the length of the session all move the target.

Is the 2% dehydration rule real?

The 2 percent rule is real in some settings and shaky in others. A 2013 meta-analysis found a 2 percent or greater body mass loss cut fixed-intensity lab performance by about 1.9 percent, but did not change self-paced time-trial performance.[2]

The review pooled 15 articles, 28 effect estimates and 122 subjects. In time-trial protocols, which look more like real competition, dehydration changed power output by just 0.09 percent, a non-significant difference. In fixed-intensity protocols the drop was 1.91 percent.[2] The author concluded that dehydration of 4 percent of body mass or less is very unlikely to impair time-trial performance, and suggested that drinking according to thirst is a reasonable approach.

Two cautions. These are laboratory endurance studies, so they cannot tell you what happens in a fourth-quarter sprint, a tournament weekend or a hot gym. And NATA still recommends keeping body mass loss below 2 percent.[1] I read the two together: avoid big losses, but do not panic over a small one.

Should athletes drink to thirst or follow a plan?

Drinking to thirst is a reasonable default during practice when you do not know your sweat rate. NATA says it helps prevent overdrinking, while competitive athletes chasing peak performance may need a plan built on measured sweat rate instead.[1]

NATA also notes that thirst can be delayed or ignored during exercise, which it calls voluntary dehydration.[1] That is why it is worth knowing your own numbers. The 2015 exercise-associated hyponatremia consensus statement sits on the other side of the same coin: it says using the innate thirst mechanism to guide fluid consumption is a strategy that should limit drinking in excess, and advises drinking palatable fluids when thirsty.[3]

How do you find your sweat rate?

Weigh yourself before and after a workout. Sweat loss equals pre-exercise body mass minus post-exercise body mass, plus the fluid you drank, minus any urine, then divided by the time you trained. Run it during a normal practice so the number reflects your real routine.[1]

NATA reports adult sweat rates from 0.5 to 4.0 L per hour, which is an eightfold spread.[1] Do the test a few times, in cool and hot conditions, and ask your athletic trainer to help you read it. For daily checks, the statement points to first-morning urine, urine color and thirst as supporting signals.[1]

Can athletes drink too much water?

Yes, you can. NATA warns that drinking more than you sweat raises the risk of exercise-associated hyponatremia, a drop in blood sodium that can be serious. Gaining body mass during exercise is the warning sign the statement tells athletes to avoid.[1]

This is why "chug as much as you can" is bad advice. The 2015 consensus statement frames thirst as a safeguard against excess drinking.[3] If you feel unwell during a long event after drinking heavily, tell a trainer right away.

How much should you drink after practice?

When you have under 4 hours before your next session, NATA suggests replacing 100 to 150 percent of the fluid you lost, because urine output rises after exercise. With 12 or more hours to recover, normal eating and drinking is generally enough.[1]

The statement also recommends getting food and fluid in within about 2 hours to replace fluid, electrolytes, carbohydrates and protein.[1] That matters most on tournament days and two-a-days.

Do athletes need sodium in their drinks?

Sometimes. NATA says sweat sodium ranges from about 10 to 100 mEq per liter, that activity under 1 hour usually needs only water, and that athletes with high sweat sodium and sweat rates above 2.5 L per hour may benefit from sodium during activity.[1]

The statement says sodium plans should be individualized and practiced beforehand. FAQ

How much water should athletes drink in a day?

There is no single number that applies to everyone. The NATA position statement says to start exercise well hydrated and to keep body mass loss under 2 percent during exercise. Use thirst, urine color and your own sweat rate to guide you.

Is it bad to drink too much water during practice?

It can be. The NATA statement warns that drinking more than you sweat raises the risk of exercise-associated hyponatremia, a dangerous drop in blood sodium. A 2015 consensus statement supports drinking when thirsty to limit excess drinking.

Does dehydration of 2 percent really hurt performance?

It depends on the setting. A 2013 meta-analysis found a 2 percent or greater loss reduced fixed-intensity lab performance, but not time-trial performance, where athletes set their own pace.

How do I calculate my sweat rate?

Weigh yourself before and after a workout, add the fluid you drank, subtract any urine, and divide by the minutes you trained. The NATA statement gives the same formula and reports adult sweat rates from 0.5 to 4.0 liters per hour.

Do I need electrolytes for every practice?

Not always. The NATA statement says activities under an hour usually need only water, and that sodium needs depend on how much sodium and fluid you lose, which varies a lot between athletes.

Does QUBE replace drinking water during practice?

No. One QUBE mixed into 12 to 16 oz of water is part of a morning routine, and its electrolytes are there for the hydration pillar. You still drink during long or hot sessions.
