Beta-alanine for repeated sprints: does it work?

I play basketball, so repeated sprints are my whole game: down the floor, back on defense, again and again. Beta-alanine is in every QUBE, so I wanted to know what the research says about beta-alanine for repeated sprints. The honest answer is not what the supplement aisle would tell you.

Does beta-alanine help repeated sprints?

Probably not, based on the best pooled evidence so far. A 2026 meta-analysis of 17 randomized trials found no significant effect of beta-alanine on mean sprint performance, peak performance or fatigue. The authors rated the certainty of the evidence as low to very low.[1]

Repeated sprint ability means how well you can repeat short, all-out sprints with little rest between them. Think a fast break, a soccer midfielder's runs or a lacrosse shift. Beta-alanine is a different tool, and it is worth knowing which job it was built for.

Where does beta-alanine help?

Beta-alanine has the best support for hard efforts lasting roughly 1 to 4 minutes. The ISSN position stand says it raises muscle carnosine, which buffers acid, and that doses of 4 to 6 g a day for at least 2 to 4 weeks improve performance in that window.[3]

A 2017 meta-analysis of 40 studies and 1,461 participants found a small overall benefit (effect size 0.18). For efforts of 0.5 to 10 minutes, the effect was larger for exercise capacity (0.50) than for performance (0.11, a range that included zero).[2] So even where beta-alanine works, the benefit is modest.

What did the 2026 meta-analysis find?

Liang and colleagues pooled 17 randomized trials of at least 2 weeks of beta-alanine versus placebo in healthy people. Doses ran 3.2 to 6.4 g a day for 3 to 10 weeks. None of the three sprint outcomes showed a significant effect.[1]

Dose, duration, exercise type and training status did not change the result. The authors flagged limits: many trials were small, five had high risk of bias, and most participants were male.[1] That is why I read this as a strong caution, not a closed case.

Why might beta-alanine miss on short sprints?

The authors suggest a mismatch. Beta-alanine builds carnosine, which buffers acid, while repeated short sprints may be limited more by how fast your muscles restore phosphocreatine between efforts.[1] This is their proposed explanation, not something the trials tested directly.

What about creatine and repeated sprints?

A 2022 meta-analysis of 14 trials found short-term creatine raised mean power output across repeated sprints (effect 0.61). It found no significant effect on peak power or fatigue, and body mass rose by about 0.79 kg.[4]

Those trials used about 20 g a day for 3 to 7 days, a loading protocol. A single 5 g serving is not the same thing, so I am not claiming this result for QUBE. For more on the loading question, see creatine loading: do you need to load creatine? and our overview of creatine for athletes.

What should a team-sport athlete take from this?

Do not expect beta-alanine to make your sprints faster or your fatigue lower. Treat it as a daily habit that the research supports for sustained hard efforts of a minute or more, and lean on training, sleep and creatine for the rest.

If you want the full picture on how the two performance ingredients fit together, read creatine and beta-alanine together: does it work? Curious about jumping? See supplements for vertical jump. If you are new to beta-alanine, the tingling is normal and manageable.

FAQ

Does beta-alanine improve repeated sprint ability?

A 2026 meta-analysis of 17 randomized trials found no significant effect on mean sprint performance, peak performance or fatigue. The authors rated the certainty of evidence as low to very low, so the result is a caution, not a final verdict.

What kind of exercise does beta-alanine help?

The ISSN position stand says beta-alanine improves performance in efforts lasting roughly 1 to 4 minutes. A 2017 meta-analysis found a small overall benefit, strongest in exercise lasting 0.5 to 10 minutes.

Does creatine help repeated sprints?

A 2022 meta-analysis of 14 trials found creatine raised mean power output across repeated sprints, but not peak power or fatigue. Those trials used loading doses of about 20 g a day for 3 to 7 days, so they are not the same as a 5 g daily serving.
