Creatine strength gains: what 69 trials show

By Quinn, Founder of QUBE

Published Nov 23, 2026Updated Nov 23, 2026Performance6 minute read

Key takeaways

  • A 2025 meta-analysis of 69 randomized trials (1,937 adults) linked creatine to small but statistically significant gains in bench press and squat.[1]
  • Pooled averages were +1.43 kg on bench press and +5.64 kg on squat. Handgrip and leg press were not significant.[1]
  • Significant results showed up in younger adults and males. The authors say more trials in women and older adults are needed.[1]
  • The ISSN describes 3 to 5 g a day of creatine monohydrate for maintenance.[2]

Creatine is the one supplement almost every strength coach will nod at. But how many pounds is it actually worth? I wanted real numbers, so I read the newest big review. The short version on creatine strength gains: they are real, they are modest, and they are not the same for everyone.

Does creatine increase strength?

Yes, modestly. A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis pooled 69 randomized trials with 1,937 adults (ages 18 to 80) and found creatine was linked to small but statistically significant gains in bench press, squat, vertical jump and Wingate peak power compared with control.[1]

About 95% of those trials used creatine monohydrate, the form in QUBE. The authors also note the effects are not uniform across all measures, which matters for the next section.[1]

How much strength does creatine add?

Pooled averages were 1.43 kg (about 3 lb) on bench or chest press and 5.64 kg (about 12 lb) on squat, plus 1.48 cm on vertical jump and 47.81 W on Wingate peak power. These are averages across studies of different lengths and designs, not a promise.[1]

Who gets the biggest creatine strength gains?

In this analysis, younger adults and males. Younger adults showed significant gains in bench (1.81 kg), leg press (8.30 kg) and squat (6.46 kg), while older adults did not. Males showed significant gains in leg press, squat, vertical jump and Wingate power, which were not observed in females.[1]

That does not mean creatine does nothing for women or older athletes. The authors call for more well-powered trials in those groups, and most trials enrolled younger men. I go deeper in creatine for female athletes.[1]

How much creatine do you need for strength gains?

The ISSN position stand describes 3 to 5 g a day of creatine monohydrate for maintenance, and notes that a loading phase is one option to fill muscle stores faster.[2] Consistency matters more than clock time, which I cover in when to take creatine and creatine loading.

What are the limits of this research?

The effects were small, not uniform across measures, and the authors flag mixed leg press results and a need for more trials in women and older adults.[1] A meta-analysis averages many different studies, so it cannot predict your own squat number.

Creatine is not magic. It is a small, consistent add-on to real training, sleep and food.

FAQ

Does creatine increase strength?

In most outcomes, yes, modestly. A 2025 meta-analysis of 69 randomized trials found creatine supplementation was linked to small but statistically significant gains in bench press (1.43 kg) and squat (5.64 kg). Handgrip and leg press results were not significant.

How much strength does creatine add?

Across the pooled trials, bench press rose by about 1.43 kg (roughly 3 lb) and squat by about 5.64 kg (roughly 12 lb) compared with control. These are averages across studies of different lengths, so your own result will vary.

Does creatine work for women and older adults?

The 2025 meta-analysis reported its significant sex-specific results in males and did not observe them in females. Gains were significant in younger adults but not older adults. The authors say more well-powered trials in women and older adults are needed.

How much creatine do you need for strength?

The ISSN position stand describes 3 to 5 g a day of creatine monohydrate for maintenance. About 95% of the trials in the 2025 meta-analysis used creatine monohydrate.
