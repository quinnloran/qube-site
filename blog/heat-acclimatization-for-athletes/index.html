Heat acclimatization for athletes: how long it takes

Preseason, summer tournaments and early fall practices all have one thing in common: they can be hot. Heat acclimatization for athletes is how your body adjusts to that, and it does not happen overnight. Here is what the research says about how long it takes, what actually changes, and how to think about hydration while you adapt.

What is heat acclimatization?

Heat acclimatization is the set of adaptations your body makes after repeated exposure to hot conditions outdoors. Heat acclimation is the same idea in a controlled setting, like a heat chamber. Both aim to help you tolerate and perform in the heat.[1]

Think of it as your body learning the conditions. The more regular the exposure, and the more you move while you are in it, the more your body adapts. The flip side is that the first few hot days of a season are when you are least prepared.

How long does heat acclimatization take?

Most heat adaptations develop within 7 to 10 days of regular heat training, and a block of about 2 weeks is a common recommendation. That comes from an applied review written for elite endurance athletes, so treat the exact numbers as a guide, not a rule for every sport.[4]

The same review suggests sessions that include at least 60 to 90 minutes of exercise in the heat. The 2015 National Athletic Trainers' Association (NATA) position statement describes introducing heat exposure gradually over about 7 to 14 days before the season. It also flags the first two to three weeks of preseason as the highest-risk period, especially in equipment-intensive sports.[3]

The practical takeaway is to ramp up. Your coaches and athletic trainer should set the plan, and you should not try to make up for lost time on day one.

What changes in your body during heat acclimatization?

In a 2022 systematic review of 29 studies and 561 healthy adults, seasonal heat acclimatization was linked to a higher sweat rate, a lower sweat sodium concentration and a lower heart rate in a heat test. Core temperature at rest dropped slightly in two studies.[1]

Seasonal heat acclimatization at a glance
Change reportedWhat the review found
Sweat rateHigher in 11 studies, by 0.03 to 0.53 liters per hour
Sweat sodium concentrationLower in 6 studies, by 22% to 59%
Heart rate in a heat testLower by 3 to 8 beats per minute in 2 studies
Resting core temperatureLower by 0.16 °C in 2 studies

The fine print matters. These were seasonal changes across a summer (mean daytime wet-bulb globe temperature about 25 °C), most studies were in healthy males, and reporting varied a lot. The size of the changes depended on how long and how hard people were active outdoors.[1]

Does heat acclimatization improve performance?

A 2019 meta-analysis of 11 randomized trials with 215 participants found better time trial performance after heat acclimatization than without it (standardized mean difference 0.50, 95% CI 0.03 to 0.97). The interval is wide, so the true effect could be small.[2]

The same analysis found no significant differences in VO2max, core temperature or plasma volume between groups. The authors concluded that acclimatization may improve tolerance to discomfort in the heat without necessarily improving the physiology markers usually tied to performance.[2] Participants averaged 26 years old and 91% were men, so apply it to your sport with care.

How should you hydrate while you adapt?

Start every hot session hydrated, and aim to keep body mass loss under 2 to 3% during exercise. The applied review behind that number also warns that overdrinking can cause hyponatremia, so more is not always better. It was written for elite endurance athletes, so ask your athletic trainer what fits your sport.[4]

Because the 2022 review linked acclimatization to a higher sweat rate and lower sweat sodium concentration, your needs in week two can differ from week one. A simple habit: weigh yourself before and after a hot practice and notice the trend. And if you ever see an athlete collapse or act confused in the heat, treat it as an emergency, call for help and start cooling right away.[4]

FAQ

How many days does heat acclimatization take?

A 2019 applied review for elite athletes says most adaptations develop within 7 to 10 days and recommends a block of about 2 weeks. The 2015 NATA position statement describes gradual heat exposure over about 7 to 14 days before the season.

Does heat acclimatization change how much you sweat?

In a 2022 systematic review of seasonal acclimatization, 11 studies reported a higher sweat rate (by 0.03 to 0.53 liters per hour) and 6 reported lower sweat sodium concentration (by 22% to 59%). That means your fluid and sodium needs can shift as you adapt.

Will heat acclimatization make me faster in the heat?

A 2019 meta-analysis of 11 randomized trials (215 participants) found better time trial performance after heat acclimatization, but a wide confidence interval. The authors suggest it may improve tolerance to discomfort more than the usual physiology markers.

Does QUBE replace heat acclimatization or water?

No. QUBE is a daily powder you mix into 12 to 16 oz of water as part of your morning routine. It includes rebalanced electrolytes (sodium, potassium and magnesium), but it does not replace gradual heat exposure, drinking enough, or your athletic trainer's heat plan.

What should I do if an athlete collapses or seems confused in the heat?

Treat it as an emergency. Call emergency services and begin cooling right away. Exertional heat stroke is a medical emergency, and the sooner cooling starts, the better the outcome. Talk with your athletic trainer about your team's heat plan.
