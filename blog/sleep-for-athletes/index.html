Sleep for athletes: how much do you need?

By Quinn, Founder of QUBE

Published Nov 4, 2026Updated Nov 4, 2026Recovery5 minute read

  • A 2022 meta-analysis of 69 publications and 959 participants found sleep loss lowered physical performance by a mean of 7.56%, with very mixed results across studies.
  • Training, travel, competition and stress are the usual reasons athletes sleep badly, and most of them can be planned around.

Everyone talks about what to take. Not enough people talk about what happens when you only got five hours before a morning lift. This post covers sleep for athletes: how much you need, what the research says about losing it, and the habits worth copying. I read the studies so you do not have to.

How much sleep do athletes need?

Healthy adults are advised to get 7 to 9 hours a night, and teenagers 8 to 10. A 2021 consensus review says experts suspect athletes need more to recover from training and competition, but it rejects a one-size-fits-all number and recommends an individual approach.[1]

The same review treats habitually sleeping under 7 hours as inadequate. In practice, that means your number is the amount that leaves you recovered and alert, not a figure from a poster. If you are a student-athlete juggling early practices and late classes, that can be harder than it sounds.

How does sleep loss affect athletic performance?

On average, sleep loss lowers physical performance. A 2022 systematic review and meta-analysis of 69 publications (959 participants, 89% male) found a mean performance change of -7.56% after sleep loss, though the studies disagreed a lot with one another.[2]

Two cautions. The groups were mostly male, and the results varied widely between studies. The 2021 consensus review adds that a full night without sleep reduces performance, but the effect of losing a little sleep for one to three nights, which is more like real life, is still unclear.[1]

Why do athletes struggle to sleep?

Sport itself gets in the way. The consensus review lists training, travel and competition as sport-specific risks, and stress, anxiety and being female as non-sport factors that shape athletes' sleep.[1]

Jet lag typically resolves at about one day per time zone crossed, according to the review, and travel fatigue from hectic trips is a separate problem. If you have a late game or a long bus ride coming, plan your sleep around it instead of hoping for the best.

Does sleeping more help athletes?

Possibly. The consensus review describes one study in collegiate basketball players who spent 10 hours in bed for 5 to 7 weeks and reported better reaction time, sprint times and shooting accuracy. It also says more research is needed on sleep extension and napping.[1]

I play college basketball, so that one got my attention. It is a small study described second-hand in the review, so I treat it as a reason to protect sleep, not a promise. The review also warns that athletes with a possible sleep disorder should be screened before being told to sleep more, because that advice can backfire.

What habits help athletes sleep better?

Boring ones. The 2021 consensus review recommends a cool, dark, quiet bedroom, morning light, a relaxing pre-bed routine, and avoiding caffeine, alcohol and heavy meals close to bedtime.[1]

  • Keep naps short. The review suggests under 30 minutes, roughly 1 to 4 p.m., and skipping naps if you struggle to sleep at night.
  • Do not lie awake in bed for long stretches. The review lists this as something to avoid.
  • Plan for travel. Expect about a day of adjustment per time zone, and build rest into the schedule.
  • Check in on your own number. If you feel flat on 7 hours, you may need more.

Persistent trouble sleeping is a question for a doctor or athletic trainer, not a supplement aisle.

FAQ

How many hours of sleep do athletes need?

General guidance is 7 to 9 hours a night for adults and 8 to 10 for teenagers. A 2021 consensus review says experts suspect athletes may need more, and it recommends an individual approach based on how much sleep you personally need to feel recovered.

Does losing sleep hurt athletic performance?

Yes, on average. A 2022 meta-analysis of 69 publications found sleep loss lowered performance by a mean of 7.56%, with larger drops the longer people were awake before exercising. Results varied a lot between studies, so the size of the effect for any one athlete is uncertain.

Is a nap good for athletes?

It can be. The 2021 consensus review suggests keeping naps under 30 minutes and taking them in the early afternoon, roughly 1 to 4 p.m., to limit grogginess. It also advises skipping naps if you struggle to fall asleep at night.

Does QUBE help you sleep?

No. QUBE is a morning powder and I make no sleep claim for it. Sleep comes from your routine, schedule and environment. QUBE is meant to sit alongside good sleep habits, not replace them.

When should an athlete see a doctor about sleep?

If you regularly lie awake, wake up unrefreshed or feel sleepy during the day, talk with a doctor or athletic trainer. The 2021 consensus review notes that people with possible sleep disorders should be screened before trying to sleep more.
