Knowing how to read a supplement label is a skill every athlete should have. I am a college athlete, and the label is the first thing I check before I put anything in my glass. Here is the process I use, what the research says about label accuracy, and where labels leave gaps.

What is on a supplement label?

A supplement label has a Supplement Facts panel listing the serving size, the amount of each ingredient per serving, and any other ingredients. The panel is where you learn what you are actually taking, so it matters more than the front of the package.

The front of the package is marketing. The Supplement Facts panel is the part that tells you what is in each serving. The authors of a 2023 JAMA Network Open study note that the FDA does not preapprove supplement ingredients for safety or efficacy.[1] That puts more of the checking on you.

How do you read the Supplement Facts panel?

Read it in four steps: check the serving size, check the amount of each active ingredient per serving, read the other ingredients, and look for any testing or certification mark. Each step takes a few seconds.

  • Serving size. Every number on the panel is per serving. A scoop and a stick pack are very different amounts, so confirm what one serving is.
  • Amount per serving. Look for a number next to each active ingredient, in grams or milligrams.
  • Other ingredients. This is where sweeteners, flavors and colors show up. If you avoid artificial sweeteners or colors, read this part.
  • Testing marks. A third-party certification mark can tell you the product was independently checked. See the testing section below.

Does the label dose match the research?

It can differ a lot, so check. The International Society of Sports Nutrition (ISSN) lists a 3 to 5 g daily maintenance amount for creatine, while beta-alanine studies typically use 4 to 6 g a day. A label amount only helps if you know the studied amount.

Are supplement labels accurate?

Not always. In a 2023 JAMA Network Open case series of 57 sports supplements, 23 (40%) had no detectable amount of the labeled ingredient, and only 6 (11%) were within 10% of the labeled amount.[1]

The same study found that 7 of 57 products (12%) contained at least one FDA-prohibited ingredient.[1] Two limits matter here. The products were bought online and labeled as containing one of five botanical stimulant or anabolic ingredients, not creatine or the other ingredients in QUBE. And the authors note a small sample with one sample per brand. So the result is a warning about a risky corner of the market, not a verdict on every supplement.

Why does third-party testing matter for athletes?

The International Olympic Committee (IOC) consensus statement lists inadvertent ingestion of banned substances as a known risk of some supplements. Independent testing is one way to lower that risk, and the IOC also recommends expert advice before you start.[2]

The same statement says only a few performance supplements, including caffeine, creatine, certain buffering agents and nitrate, have good evidence of benefit, and that you should trial a supplement in training before using it in competition.[2] That is sound advice at any level. If you play for a team, talk with your athletic trainer before adding anything new.

What are the red flags on a supplement label?

These are the things I look for as a buyer. None of them proves a product is bad, but each one is a reason to slow down and ask questions before you buy.

  • Blends without individual amounts. If a label shows one total for several ingredients, you cannot compare any of them with the research.
  • Stimulant and anabolic claims. These were the categories in the 2023 study that missed the label most often.[1]
  • No testing information. If a brand says nothing about independent testing, ask them.
  • Big promises. Words like "miracle" or "guaranteed" are marketing, not evidence.
  • Artificial sweeteners or colors you want to avoid. Check the other ingredients list.

FAQ

How do you read a supplement label?

Start with the serving size, then check the amount of each active ingredient per serving, then read the other ingredients list. Compare each amount with the dose used in the research, and look for independent testing.

Are supplement labels always accurate?

Not always. In a 2023 JAMA Network Open study of 57 sports supplements, 40% had no detectable amount of the labeled ingredient. The products were stimulant and anabolic botanicals, so the result does not apply to every supplement.

What is a proprietary blend?

A proprietary blend lists several ingredients under one total weight without showing the amount of each. Because you cannot see individual doses, you cannot compare them with the research. I prefer labels that show every amount.

Does third-party testing matter for athletes?

The IOC consensus statement lists inadvertent ingestion of banned substances as a known risk of some supplements. Independent testing is one way to reduce that risk. Check with your athletic trainer or team staff before taking any supplement.
