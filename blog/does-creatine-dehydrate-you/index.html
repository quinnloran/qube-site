If you have ever heard a teammate say creatine dries you out and gives you cramps, you have heard one of the oldest supplement rumors in sports. The question does creatine dehydrate you has been studied directly, and the answer is more reassuring than the locker room version. Here is what the research says, and where it stops.

Does creatine dehydrate you?

No, the research does not show that creatine dehydrates you. A 2009 systematic review of 10 studies found no evidence that creatine hinders the body's ability to dissipate heat or its hydration status, and the ISSN reports supplementation appears safe and well tolerated in the studied range.[1][3]

The review searched five databases and included studies that measured heat tolerance or hydration in people taking creatine.[1] That is the key point: the rumor was tested head-on, and it did not hold up. The caveat is that these are mostly short controlled trials in healthy adults, so they cannot speak to every person or every medical situation.

Why does creatine change body weight?

Creatine can raise body weight because muscle holds more water, not because it pulls water away from the rest of you. A 2025 narrative review notes that loading with 20 g a day for 5 days raised body weight by about 1 to 3 kg, mostly from total body water.[4]

That review also reports studies finding no difference in sweat rate or plasma volume with creatine, though it is a narrative review that summarizes other trials, so I treat it as context rather than proof.[4] The loading protocol is the part to notice. The ISSN describes a quick-load of about 0.3 g per kg of body weight a day for 5 to 7 days, then 3 to 5 g a day, and says 3 to 5 g a day alone raises muscle stores over about 3 to 4 weeks.[3] QUBE has no loading phase.

Does creatine cause muscle cramps?

The research does not support the idea that creatine causes muscle cramps. A 2008 review in the British Journal of Sports Medicine described the belief as a fallacy driven by media claims and anecdotes, and found little evidence that creatine adds risk when exercising in the heat.[2]

The authors also pointed out that an older American College of Sports Medicine statement from 2000 advised some athletes to avoid creatine in hot conditions, and argued the newer evidence should be considered in future guidance.[2] If you do get cramps in practice or games, that is worth raising with your athletic trainer, because the studies simply do not point the finger at creatine.

Is creatine safe to take in the heat?

In controlled research, creatine has not been shown to impair exercise in the heat. The 2009 systematic review found no evidence of impaired heat dissipation or fluid balance, and the 2008 review concluded that creatine in the heat poses little additional risk.[1][2]

I am not saying creatine makes you better in the heat. Both reviews are about whether it causes harm, and neither is a reason to skip normal heat habits like drinking fluids, easing into hot practices and listening to your trainer. If you have a kidney or other health condition, or take medication, check with a doctor first.

How much water should you drink with creatine?

The studies above do not set a special water target for creatine, so there is no research-backed number to quote. Drink fluids the way you normally would for training, and mix your creatine into a full glass rather than taking it dry.

That is a practical habit, not a proven rule. Hydration also depends on sweat, sodium and the climate you train in, which is why the electrolytes in your routine matter as much as the creatine.

FAQ

Does creatine dehydrate you?

No. A 2009 systematic review of 10 studies found no evidence that creatine hinders heat dissipation or hydration status. Check with a doctor or athletic trainer if you have a health condition or take medication.

Does creatine cause muscle cramps?

The research does not support that. A 2008 review in the British Journal of Sports Medicine called the cramp belief a myth driven by anecdotes and media claims.

Does creatine cause water retention?

Creatine can increase body water, mostly inside the body, especially during a loading phase. A 2025 narrative review notes a 1 to 3 kg weight rise with 20 g a day for 5 days.

Do I need to load creatine?

No. The ISSN says 3 to 5 g a day raises muscle stores over about 3 to 4 weeks, while loading gets there faster. QUBE has 5 g per serving and no loading phase.

Is creatine safe to take in hot weather?

Controlled research has not shown that it is a problem. Keep normal heat habits, and ask your athletic trainer about your own situation.

Does QUBE contain creatine?

Yes. Each QUBE has 5 g of creatine monohydrate plus beta-alanine, tart cherry extract, curcumin and electrolytes.
