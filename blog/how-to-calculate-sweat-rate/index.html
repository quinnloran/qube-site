How to calculate sweat rate: an athlete's guide

By Quinn, Founder of QUBE

Published Nov 18, 2026Updated Nov 18, 2026Hydration5 minute read

Key takeaways

  • You can calculate sweat rate with a scale: weight before, weight after, plus what you drank, divided by hours.[1]
  • Reported adult sweat rates run from about 0.5 to 4.0 L/h, so a teammate's numbers may be nothing like yours.[1]
  • The NATA says to aim for a body mass loss of less than 2% by the end of exercise.[1]

I have played through August two-a-days and winter gyms with the heat cranked, and my water bottle strategy was basically a guess. Then I learned you can measure it. Knowing how to calculate sweat rate turns hydration from a feeling into a number you can plan around. Here is the method and what the research says to do with it.

What is sweat rate?

Sweat rate is how much fluid you lose through sweating per hour of exercise, usually in liters per hour (L/h). The NATA position statement reports adult sweat rates of roughly 0.5 to 4.0 L/h and says these large differences call for individual hydration plans, not population estimates.[1]

Sweat also carries sodium. The same statement reports sweat sodium concentrations of roughly 10 to 100 mEq/L, which is why two athletes can finish the same practice in very different shape.[1]

How do you calculate sweat rate?

Weigh yourself before and after exercise, add the fluid you drank, subtract any urine, and divide by the hours you trained. The NATA formula is sweat rate (L/h) = sweat loss (L) / exercise duration (h), where sweat loss is pre-exercise mass minus post-exercise mass, plus fluid consumed, minus urine.[1]

  1. Weigh in before. Use a reliable floor scale, ideally after using the bathroom, and note the number in kilograms.
  2. Train as normal and note exactly how much you drink (in liters).
  3. Weigh in after. Same scale, same clothing situation, and note your time.
  4. Do the math. Mass lost in kg plus liters drunk is your sweat loss. Divide by hours.

The paper's formula uses kilograms and liters together, so the math treats 1 kg of body mass lost as about 1 L of fluid. The statement also says a valid, reliable scale matters, and that a euhydrated baseline comes from 3 consecutive days of morning weigh-ins at the same time.[1]

What does a sweat rate calculation look like?

Here is a made-up example, not data from any study. An athlete weighs 80.0 kg before and 79.0 kg after a 90-minute practice, drinks 0.5 L and skips the bathroom. Sweat loss is 1.0 + 0.5 = 1.5 L, and 1.5 L divided by 1.5 h gives 1.0 L/h.

Hypothetical example
StepNumber
Mass lost80.0 kg minus 79.0 kg = 1.0 kg
Fluid drunk0.5 L
Sweat loss1.0 + 0.5 = 1.5 L
Sweat rate1.5 L / 1.5 h = 1.0 L/h

Notice the athlete ended 1.0 kg lighter, or 1.25% of starting body mass, even after drinking.

How much body weight loss is too much?

The NATA says athletes should aim for a body mass loss of less than 2% by the end of exercise, starting from a hydrated state (within +1% to -1% of baseline). It notes thermoregulation is compromised by hypohydration greater than 1%, and exertional heat illness risk rises at levels greater than 3%.[1]

For an 80 kg athlete, 2% is 1.6 kg. If your post-practice weigh-in lands past that line, your plan needs more fluid.

Why does sweat rate change from day to day?

Sweat rate depends on exercise intensity, environment, heat acclimatization, fitness and body size. A review of sweating methodology also lists protective equipment, sex, age, maturation, diet and hydration, and concludes sweat testing can guide replacement when data are collected and interpreted properly.[2]

So run the test more than once: a hot afternoon practice and an air-conditioned lift will not match. That is why one number is a start, not a rule.

Can you just drink to thirst?

Thirst is a useful but imperfect cue. According to the NATA, thirst typically rises near 2% hypohydration, and drinking only when thirsty may compromise performance for competitive athletes. If you do not know your sweat rate, drinking to thirst is considered a safe way to avoid overdrinking.[1]

The statement also says to replace only the fluid lost in sweat and avoid gaining fluid weight, to reduce the risk of exercise-associated hyponatremia.[1] The goal is matching your losses, not chugging. FAQ

How do you calculate your sweat rate?

Weigh yourself before and after a workout, add the fluid you drank, subtract any urine, then divide by hours of exercise. The NATA formula is sweat rate (L/h) = sweat loss (L) / exercise duration (h), with body mass in kilograms and fluid in liters.

What is a normal sweat rate for athletes?

There is no single normal. The NATA position statement reports adult sweat rates of roughly 0.5 to 4.0 L/h, and says body size, environment, intensity and heat acclimatization all matter, so individual data beat population estimates.

The NATA says to aim for a body mass loss under 2% by the end of exercise. It also says exertional heat illness risk rises at levels greater than 3% and that thermoregulation is compromised by hypohydration greater than 1%.
