Urine color chart for athletes: how to use it

Every locker room has seen the poster above the toilet: pale is good, dark is bad. A urine color chart for athletes is the cheapest hydration check there is, so I wanted to know how much it can really tell you. The short version: it is a useful clue, and a weak verdict when you use it alone.

What is a urine color chart for athletes?

A urine color chart is a strip of numbered shades, from pale to dark, that you compare against a urine sample. It estimates urine concentration, meaning how much dissolved material is packed into the fluid. Darker usually means more concentrated, which can point to drinking too little.

The National Athletic Trainers' Association (NATA) calls urine color a popular method because it is noninvasive, inexpensive and reliable, and says it also works as a way to teach athletes to check themselves.[1]

How accurate is a urine color chart for athletes?

Decent, not exact. A 2021 study had 189 people score their own urine on two charts. Against urine specific gravity (a measure of concentration), they were classified correctly about 77% of the time. Against osmolality, a second concentration measure, accuracy was about 64 to 66%.[3]

So in that study, roughly 1 in 4 samples landed in the wrong bucket even on the friendlier measure. Scoring also happened under controlled lighting, so your bathroom may do worse.

The older groundwork agrees. Lawrence Armstrong's 1994 paper found urine color was strongly correlated with those two urine measures, but not with the blood measures tested. The authors said color is fine for athletic and field settings where a close estimate is enough, and not for work that needs precision.[2]

How do you check your hydration urine color correctly?

Use your first morning urine, collect it in a clear container, and compare it with the chart in good light without shadows. NATA says color in the toilet bowl is not reliable because water dilutes the sample, and that first morning samples give more valid hydration measurements.[1]

  • Same time daily. Samples later in the day can be skewed by what you just drank, ate or did in training.[1]
  • Same light. A dim dorm bathroom and a bright training room can make one sample look different.
  • Look for a pattern. One odd morning matters less than several dark ones in a row.

What number should you aim for? In the 2021 study, the best cut-offs for separating dilute from concentrated urine were pale: 1 or at most 2, depending on the chart and lab measure.[3] Charts differ, so do not carry a number from one chart to another without checking.

What can throw off your urine color?

Timing, rapid drinking and possibly some vitamins. Spot samples can be confounded by fluid, food and exercise.[1] The 2021 chart study also noted some evidence that riboflavin (vitamin B2) makes color scoring less accurate, though its supplement users and non-users were classified about equally well.[3]

If you take a multivitamin or other supplements, read the label so a color change does not catch you off guard. My guide on how to read a supplement label walks through the panel.

One serious exception: very dark, brown or cola-colored urine is not a "drink more water" problem. NATA flags the darkest shade on its 8-color scale as a sign of muscle damage that needs prompt medical referral.[1] Tell your athletic trainer or a doctor right away.

How do weight, urine color and thirst work together?

The idea is called WUT: weight, urine, thirst. A 2021 study of 43 college-aged adults found that when all three flagged a problem, hypohydration (low body water) was likely, and with no flags, good hydration was highly likely. One or two flags were indeterminate.[4]

The flags used in the WUT study
CheckFlag in the study
WeightDown more than 1% from your usual morning weight
Urine colorDarker than 5 on an 8-color chart
Thirst5 or higher on a 1 to 9 scale

Read the fine print. All three flags together rarely gave a false alarm (about 96% specificity against urine specific gravity), but they caught only about 9% of the samples that test marked as underhydrated. The study was funded by CamelBak, a bottle company, and its reference points were urine tests, not blood.[4]

You may notice the color flag here (darker than 5) is far less strict than the pale cut-offs in the chart study. The two used different methods, which is exactly why I would not treat any one shade as a pass or fail line.

For the weight piece, NATA says the baseline should be an average of at least 3 consecutive days when you are well hydrated.[1] After that, a quick step on the scale before breakfast gives your chart some context.

FAQ

What color should an athlete's urine be?

Paler generally means more dilute and darker means more concentrated. In a 2021 study of athletes and tactical athletes, the best cut-offs for flagging concentrated urine were quite pale, 1 or at most 2 on the charts used. No single shade proves you are hydrated.

Is clear urine a sign of good hydration?

Pale urine points to dilute urine, but it can also show up right after you drink a lot of fluid quickly. NATA notes that samples taken during the day can be confounded by fluid, exercise and food, so a first morning sample tells you more.

Can vitamins change urine color?

They can interfere with reading it. A 2021 study noted some evidence that riboflavin (vitamin B2) makes urine color scoring less accurate, although its own supplement users and non-users were classified about equally well.

When is the best time to check urine color?

First thing in the morning. The National Athletic Trainers' Association says assessing the first morning void improves the validity of hydration measurements compared with spot samples later in the day.

When should dark urine worry me?

Very dark, brown or cola-colored urine, especially after hard exercise, needs a doctor or athletic trainer right away. NATA flags the darkest shade on its 8-color scale as a sign of muscle damage that calls for prompt referral.
