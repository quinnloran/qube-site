Alcohol and Muscle Recovery: what research shows

By Quinn, Founder of QUBE

Published Nov 16, 2026Updated Nov 16, 2026Recovery4 minute read

Everyone on a college team knows the weekend. I am a college athlete too, so I wanted to see what the research says about alcohol and muscle recovery instead of guessing. This is a read of the studies, not a lecture, and it is not encouragement to drink.

Does alcohol affect muscle recovery?

It may. A 2014 review in Sports Medicine concluded that at typical athlete drinking levels, alcohol can disrupt immune and hormonal function, blood flow and muscle protein synthesis, which may impair recovery from muscle injury.[2]

The review also says the effect depends on how much you drink, when you drink relative to exercise, how long until you train again, and whether you are injured. So the honest answer is not a yes or no, it is a set of "it depends" factors.

What did the 2010 eccentric exercise study find?

Eleven healthy men did 300 maximal eccentric quadriceps contractions on two occasions. After one session they drank 1 g/kg of alcohol, and after the other they drank orange juice. Strength losses were significantly bigger after alcohol.[1]

Eccentric work, where the muscle lengthens under load, is the kind of effort that leaves you sore after a hard game or a new lift. At 36 hours, peak losses were about 34% (isometric), 40% (concentric) and 34% (eccentric) with alcohol. With orange juice they were about 12%, 28% and 19%.[1]

Soreness and creatine kinase, a blood marker of muscle damage, did not differ between conditions. That is a useful detail: you may not feel any worse, even when strength is lower.[1]

What do the reviews say about alcohol and recovery?

The 2014 review found that rehydration and glycogen restoration are affected to a lesser degree than muscle injury recovery. A 2022 narrative review reported that alcohol may impair muscle recovery after intense exercise, with men appearing more affected than women.[2][3]

The 2022 review points to several possible mechanisms, including imbalanced muscle building and breakdown pathways, reduced regeneration, and more inflammation. These are narrative reviews, so they summarize studies instead of pooling them in a meta-analysis.[3]

Does the amount of alcohol matter?

Probably. The 2014 review noted that about 0.5 g/kg of body weight after exercise is unlikely to affect most recovery processes. The 2010 study used twice that, 1 g/kg.[1][2]

Drink sizes vary a lot, and these figures are grams of ethanol per kilogram of body weight, so do not treat them as a safe number of drinks. Your athletic trainer or team doctor can talk through your own situation.

What can the research not tell us?

Quite a bit. The 2014 review focused on male athletes, the 2010 study had 11 men, and the 2022 review notes that episodic binge drinking, the most common athlete pattern, is under-represented in lab studies.[1][2][3]

We left out claims about sleep, performance in the next game and women specifically, because we did not verify studies on them for this post. FAQ

Does alcohol slow muscle recovery?

A 2014 review in Sports Medicine concluded that at typical athlete drinking levels, alcohol can disrupt immune and hormonal function, blood flow and muscle protein synthesis, which may impair recovery from muscle injury. Rehydration and glycogen restoration were affected less.

How much did alcohol reduce strength in the 2010 study?

In 11 healthy men who did 300 maximal eccentric contractions, 1 g/kg of alcohol after exercise was linked to peak strength losses of about 34% (isometric), 40% (concentric) and 34% (eccentric), versus about 12%, 28% and 19% with orange juice. Soreness and creatine kinase did not differ.

Is a small amount of alcohol after a game okay for recovery?

The 2014 review noted that about 0.5 g/kg of body weight after exercise is unlikely to affect most recovery processes. Real drinks vary a lot, and the review focused on male athletes, so this is not a green light. Ask your athletic trainer about your situation.

Does binge drinking affect athletic performance?

A 2022 narrative review reported that binge drinking is common in athletes, is under-studied in the lab, and may still impair muscle function and performance. It also noted that alcohol may impair muscle recovery after intense exercise, with men appearing more affected than women.

Can QUBE offset the effects of alcohol?

No. QUBE is a daily powder for your morning routine and it is not designed to counter alcohol. It supports your routine, and it does not replace sleep, food, fluids or smart choices.
