Supplements for Vertical Jump: What Research Shows

By Quinn, Founder of QUBE

Published Nov 2, 2026Updated Nov 2, 2026Performance6 minute read

Key takeaways

  • A 2025 analysis of 35 trials and 991 athletes found significant jump benefits for beta-alanine (SMD 0.41) and creatine (SMD 0.30) when paired with conditioning training, among the supplements it reported for jump.
  • A separate meta-analysis of 69 trials put creatine's vertical jump gain at about 1.5 cm, or roughly half an inch.
  • Caffeine, vitamin D and protein did not show a significant jump effect in that network analysis.
  • Every trial paired the supplement with training, and the evidence quality was mostly low. Think of supplements as a small add-on, not a shortcut.

I play college basketball, so I get asked about supplements for vertical jump constantly, and most of what comes up in a search is a brand selling its own product. Here is what two recent analyses actually found, with the numbers and the fine print.

Do supplements for vertical jump actually work?

A few might, by a small margin. A 2025 network meta-analysis of 35 randomized trials and 991 athletes found that creatine and beta-alanine, added to conditioning training, were the two with a statistically significant jump benefit among the supplements reported.[1]

The results are reported as a standardized mean difference (SMD), where roughly 0.2 is small and 0.5 is moderate. In every trial, the comparison group did the same training without the supplement, so these numbers describe an add-on to training, not a replacement for it.

Does creatine increase vertical jump?

Creatine plus training produced a small gain. A 2025 meta-analysis of 69 randomized trials and 1,937 participants found vertical jump improved by 1.48 cm (95% CI 0.30 to 2.66) compared with placebo, and Wingate peak power rose by about 48 W.[2]

That is roughly 0.6 of an inch, which is real but modest. Two caveats matter. First, the vertical jump results varied a lot between studies (I² of about 71%). Second, the gains were significant in males and in trained participants, while the female subgroup did not reach significance. The authors noted that few trials included women and called for more research.[2] We cover that gap in creatine for female athletes.

In the network analysis, creatine also ranked first for sprint speed (SMD -0.42, where a negative number means faster times).[1] For the basics of how creatine works in muscle, see creatine for athletes.

Does beta-alanine help you jump higher?

Possibly, but the evidence is less clear than the headline number suggests. Beta-alanine had the largest jump effect in the network analysis (SMD 0.41), yet its confidence interval was wide, and the analysis does not explain why a sub-second jump would respond.[1]

Beta-alanine's best-supported role is in hard efforts lasting about 1 to 4 minutes, according to the ISSN position stand.[4] A separate meta-analysis of 40 studies and 1,461 participants found a small overall effect (0.18), larger for exercise capacity (0.50) than for performance measures (0.11, with a confidence interval that crossed zero).[3] One idea is that beta-alanine helps athletes handle more hard work in training, but that is a hypothesis, not something these analyses tested.

On dose, the analyses I read do not give one for the jump results. The sprinter study on our Research page used 4.8 g a day for four weeks, which is more than one 2 g serving.[5] Our post on creatine and beta-alanine together goes deeper on the pair.

What about caffeine, protein and vitamin D?

None of the three showed a significant jump effect in the 2025 network analysis. That does not mean they are useless: protein ranked first for muscular strength (SMD 0.64), which is a different outcome from jumping.[1]

If you see tart cherry mentioned for jump height, that research is about recovering jump height after damaging exercise, not raising your ceiling. We break that down in tart cherry for muscle soreness.

What do these studies not tell you?

They cannot promise you a higher jump. In the network analysis, only 3 of 35 trials had a low risk of bias, most comparisons were rated low or very low certainty, and 83.9% of participants were male.[1]

Most interventions lasted under 12 weeks, doses and training programs varied, and the creatine analysis found that most included trials did not blind participants.[1][2] If you have a health condition or take medication, check with a doctor or athletic trainer before adding any supplement.

FAQ

Does creatine increase vertical jump?

A 2025 meta-analysis of 69 trials found creatine plus training added about 1.5 cm (roughly 0.6 inches) to vertical jump compared with placebo. The result varied a lot between studies, and it was significant in males and trained people but not in the female subgroup.

Is beta-alanine good for jumping?

A 2025 network meta-analysis found a small-to-moderate pooled benefit for jump performance with beta-alanine added to conditioning training, but the confidence interval was wide and most of the included trials had some risk of bias. Beta-alanine's best-supported role is in hard efforts lasting about 1 to 4 minutes.

Do supplements for vertical jump work for female athletes?

The evidence is thinner. In the 2025 creatine meta-analysis, women did not show significant gains in vertical jump, but the authors noted that few studies included women and called for more research.

Should I take creatine right before a game to jump higher?

The research does not point to a pre-game dose. Creatine works by building up in muscle over time, and a review of timing studies concluded timing around workouts should not be considered a real concern for now. See our post on when to take creatine.

No. QUBE is a daily powder, not a pre-workout, and QUBE itself has not been studied for vertical jump. Mix one QUBE into 12 to 16 oz of water as part of your morning routine.
