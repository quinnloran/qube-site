Sleep is where recovery really happens, so athletes keep asking me whether tart cherry can help. I read what has been published. The short version: tart cherry for sleep is an interesting idea with early, mixed evidence, and nobody should treat it as a fix.

Does tart cherry help you sleep?

Possibly, but the evidence is early. A 2025 systematic review found some studies reporting better sleep or higher melatonin, while its authors described the evidence as limited and heterogeneous. One small trial in elite athletes points the same cautious way.[1][2]

Tart cherry (often Montmorency) is rich in anthocyanins, the pigments that give it its deep red color. Researchers have looked at it for both exercise recovery and sleep, which is why the two topics keep showing up together.

What did the 2025 review find about tart cherry and sleep?

The review pooled 7 interventional studies. Three reported improvements in sleep measures such as duration, efficiency or time to fall asleep, three reported higher melatonin, and two reported lower inflammation markers.[1]

The authors concluded that tart cherry may support sleep quality, but that the studies differed a lot and that high-quality trials in more diverse groups are needed.[1] Read that as a reason to stay curious, not to stock up.

What happened in a trial of elite athletes?

A 2022 randomized trial gave 19 elite female field hockey players either tart cherry juice or placebo for 5 days after intermittent exercise. Melatonin and cortisol did not change, but some actigraphy sleep measures did.[2]

Specifically, the trial reported significant group-by-time differences in total time in bed, wake after sleep onset and a movement index. The authors concluded tart cherry juice may improve sleep quality in these athletes. With 10 people in one group and 9 in the other, I treat this as a signal worth testing again, not a result to build a routine around.

Does dose or timing matter?

Probably, but the research cannot say how. The 2025 review reported that the studies varied widely in dose, length and participants, which is a big reason the results are hard to compare.[1]

That also means I am not going to give you a magic amount or a bedtime recipe. If sleep is a problem for you, a doctor or athletic trainer is a better first stop than a supplement.

FAQ

Does tart cherry help you sleep?

Possibly, but the evidence is early. A 2025 systematic review of 7 studies found 3 that reported better sleep measures, and the authors called the evidence limited and inconsistent. Better trials are still needed.

Does tart cherry raise melatonin?

Some studies say yes. Three of the 7 studies in the 2025 review reported higher melatonin levels. A separate 2022 trial in elite field hockey players found no change in melatonin or cortisol, so the picture is mixed.

Is the tart cherry in QUBE meant for sleep?

No. QUBE is a morning powder, and the tart cherry in it (1,000 mg of extract standardized to anthocyanins) is there for the recovery side of the formula. The sleep studies used different products and routines, so they should not be read as a claim about QUBE.

Who should check with a doctor before using tart cherry?

Anyone with a sleep problem, a health condition or a medication should check with a doctor or athletic trainer first. Persistent trouble sleeping deserves a conversation with a professional, not a supplement.
