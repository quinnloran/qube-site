Cold weather hydration means replacing the fluid you lose when you train or play in cool and cold conditions, where you may not feel like drinking. Winter season is when my water bottle is easiest to ignore, so I went and read the studies. The short version: sweat does not stop when the temperature drops, but the research is more nuanced than "winter dehydration is a hidden emergency."

Do you sweat enough in cold weather for it to matter?

Often, yes. In a 2005 study, 17 elite soccer players trained for 90 minutes at 5 degrees C (41 F) and lost an average of 1.69 liters of sweat, while drinking only 423 milliliters. Individual sweat losses ranged from 1.06 to 2.65 liters.[3]

Cold rinks count too. Researchers tested 44 junior hockey players at one-hour on-ice practices (13.9 degrees C, about 57 F). Average sweat rate was 1.8 liters per hour, players replaced 58% of it, and one third lost more than 1% of body weight.[4]

A study published in 2026 followed 36 professional soccer players in cool conditions (about 11 degrees C, 52 F). Outfield players averaged a 1.5 liter per hour sweat rate, and 6 of them lost more than 2% of body mass in a session of about 70 minutes. One author affiliation is the Gatorade Sports Science Institute, so keep that in mind.[6]

Why are you less thirsty in cold weather?

Your thirst signal may be quieter in the cold. A 2004 lab study had small groups of men rest and exercise at 4 degrees C and 27 degrees C. In the cold, thirst was reduced by up to 40%, whether the men started hydrated or not.[2]

The researchers suggested that narrowing blood vessels in the skin may shift blood toward your core, which could dial down thirst. They framed that as a possible explanation, not a proven one.[2]

A 2014 study adds a useful wrinkle. Ten men cycled for an hour at about 0 or 25 degrees C, then recovered indoors with water available. After the cold ride they drank less (0.50 vs 0.81 liters), although thirst ratings were similar between trials. They had also sweated about half as much, and the authors concluded that drinking freely still kept hydration appropriate in both conditions.[1]

So "drinking less in the cold" is not automatically a problem. It becomes one when your sweat loss stays high and your drinking does not.

Are athletes already dehydrated before winter practice?

Some are. A 2018 study collected urine from 81 semiprofessional male soccer players right before winter training in Estonia and Latvia. By the urine concentration cutoff the authors reported, 50 of 81 players (61.7%) were hypohydrated, meaning low on body water, before practice even started.[5]

The hockey study saw something similar: more than half of the players arrived mildly hypohydrated.[4] A single urine sample is an imperfect snapshot, and the soccer authors say so. Still, what you drink in the hours before practice is worth a look.

Does cold weather dehydration hurt performance?

Less clearly than in the heat. A 2015 review concluded that hypohydration does not impair submaximal intensity aerobic performance in cool or cold environments, while the effect becomes more likely as skin temperature climbs.[7]

I think that is important to say out loud. The review is about aerobic performance, not repeated sprints, skill or decision making in your sport, and it does not mean fluid is optional. If you train indoors in a warm gym all winter, the weather outside is not your training environment anyway.

What does a simple cold weather hydration plan look like?

Measure, then drink on purpose. Weigh yourself before and after a typical practice, and track what you drank. The weight change plus the fluid you drank gives a rough picture of your sweat loss, which beats guessing from how thirsty you felt.

These are my own practical habits, not a protocol tested in the studies above:

  • Start early. Drink with breakfast and lunch instead of cramming right before practice.
  • Bring a bottle anyway. Put it on the bench even when the gym or rink feels cold.
  • Use breaks. Take a few sips at planned stoppages, not only when you notice thirst.
  • Eat after. A normal meal brings back fluid and sodium along with your food.
  • Do not overdo it. More water is not always better. Finishing practice heavier than you started is a sign you drank more than you lost.

If you get dizzy, confused or unwell, or you have a heart, kidney or blood pressure condition, talk to a doctor or athletic trainer before changing how you drink.

FAQ

Do you need to drink water in cold weather?

Yes. Athletes still sweat in the cold. In one study, 17 elite soccer players lost an average of 1.69 liters of sweat in a 90 minute session at 5 degrees C (41 F) and drank about a quarter of that.[3]

Why are you less thirsty when it is cold?

A 2004 lab study found thirst was reduced by up to 40% in the cold, at rest and during moderate exercise. The authors suggested that blood vessels narrowing in the skin may be involved. Not every study sees the same drop in thirst ratings.[2][1]

Is dehydration as big a problem in winter as in summer?

For submaximal endurance performance, a 2015 review concluded that hypohydration does not impair it in cool or cold environments, while heat is a different story. That does not cover every sport, intensity or athlete, and it is not a reason to skip drinking.[7]

Do hockey players sweat a lot in a cold rink?

They can. In a 2008 study of 44 junior hockey players, average sweat rate during a one hour on-ice practice was 1.8 liters per hour, and one third of players lost more than 1% of body weight even with fluid available.[4]

Should I add electrolytes in cold weather?

Sweat still carries sodium when it is cold. How much you need to replace depends on your sweat losses, your meals and how soon you train again, so ask your athletic trainer or doctor, especially if you have a medical condition.[3][4]
